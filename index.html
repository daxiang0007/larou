02 · Etymology

A character that means "the twelfth month"

The word itself is a small piece of the history. Tap each line to open it.

臘
Not the character for "spicy"
Là (臘/腊) is a near-homophone of the là in málà, "numbing-spicy" — but it's an entirely different character, with nothing to do with chili. It's one of the first mix-ups English speakers make when they meet the word, and one of the first things a Chinese menu will quietly correct.
Originally the name of a month, not a food
Before it described meat, 臘 named 臘月 (làyuè) — the twelfth month of the lunar calendar, deep winter. The month took its name from 臘祭 (làjì), a sacrificial rite offering the year's dried and cured meats to ancestors and household spirits, thanking them for the harvest and asking for protection into the new year.
Recorded roughly 3,000 years ago
The rite appears in ritual compendia from the Zhou dynasty (c. 1046–256 BCE), including the Rites of Zhou and the Book of Rites — among the oldest surviving descriptions of organized meat preservation anywhere in the world. Meat cured "for the 臘 rite" became 臘肉 — literally "twelfth-month meat." The name stuck long after the rite faded from daily life, which is why làròu today is made, and eaten, at any point in winter.
03 · A history

From temple offering to family freezer

Three thousand years is a long supply chain. Here's how a ritual became a pantry staple.

C. 1046–256 BCE · ZHOU DYNASTY

A sacrifice, not a snack

The earliest recorded curing is ceremonial: meat dried and salted specifically to be offered to ancestors during the làjì rite at year's end, then shared among the household as a blessing.

206 BCE – 220 CE · HAN DYNASTY

The ritual becomes a technique

As curing knowledge spread beyond temple households, its real value became obvious: a family's one winter pig, salted and hung, could feed them for months. Preservation started outpacing ceremony.

1368–1912 · MING & QING DYNASTIES

One method, many dialects

Migration and trade carried the practice into wildly different climates. Sichuan reached for chili and pine smoke; Guangdong's warm, wet winters favored wind and rice wine over any smokehouse at all. The regional styles eaten today mostly date from this stretch.

20TH CENTURY

A countdown you could see

Làròu became the visible clock of the Lunar New Year — strips of meat drying on apartment balconies and rooftops across China each December, a neighborhood-wide sign the reunion dinner was close.

TODAY

Hanging on, further from home

Vacuum-sealed and shipped worldwide, làròu now hangs in Chinatown markets from San Francisco to Flushing — carried, quite literally, by the families who grew up with it on the rafter each winter.

04 · Regional styles

Same idea, five very different smokehouses

"Làròu" is an umbrella, not a recipe. Climate and local taste pulled it in different directions.

Sichuan & Chongqing

Chili and mountain pine

Pork belly rubbed with chili and Sichuan pepper before it ever meets smoke, then hung over cypress or pine. The result carries heat, numbing spice, and campfire in one bite.

Hunan

Smoked hardest, smoked longest

Often cured over tea leaves, orange peel, or sugarcane husks for weeks, until the meat turns deep amber and the smoke flavor is unmistakably the point.

Guangdong (Cantonese)

臘肉 with no smoke at all

Marinated in rose-flavored liquor, soy, and sugar, then simply air-dried in cool winter wind — sweeter, glossier, no smokehouse required. Its sibling is 臘腸 (lap cheong), the cured sausage sold in the same string.

Yunnan

Salt from the mountain itself

Some Yunnan làròu is cured with brine drawn from ancient mountain salt wells, then smoked over pine and herbs at altitude — a cure shaped by geology as much as season.

Hakka & Jiangxi

Built to travel

Heavier salt and a drier finish — a legacy of the Hakka, a historically migratory people who needed meat that could survive the road, not just the winter.

05 · The process

Five steps, several weeks, one hook

The method varies by region, but nearly every batch of làròu passes through the same five moves.

Salt

Coarse salt, sometimes mixed with spice, is rubbed hard into fresh pork belly. Over several days it draws out moisture and begins denaturing the surface — the first and most essential defense against spoilage.

Marinate

Depending on the region, the salted meat is worked with rice wine, soy sauce, sugar, or chili — the step where a regional identity actually gets written into the meat.

Hang

Strung by a cord or hook, the meat goes up somewhere cold and dry — an eave, a balcony, a beam over the stove. Air circulation from here on does most of the remaining work.

Smoke or dry

Climate decides this step. Cold, dry mountain regions smoke over wood, tea, or citrus peel for deep flavor. Warm, humid Guangdong skips smoke entirely and trusts the winter wind alone.

Age

Two to four weeks of patience while the meat firms, darkens, and its flavor concentrates — before it's finally sliced thin and steamed, stir-fried, or simmered into a claypot rice.

06 · Why it matters

The smell that means the new year is close

"When the meat goes up on the balcony, you know the new year is coming — before anyone says a word about it."

A saying repeated across households where làròu is made every winter

Làròu's real home isn't the smokehouse — it's the reunion table. For 團年飯 (tuánniánfàn), the Lunar New Year reunion dinner, cured meat is one of the dishes a family is least willing to go without, steamed simply so the months of salt, smoke, and patience speak for themselves.

For relatives who've moved away — across a province, or across an ocean — a jar or vacuum-sealed bag of home-cured làròu is a common gift back: not a delicacy exactly, but a specific, unmistakable taste of a specific kitchen, which is a harder thing to give than it sounds.

Trying it, in the US

You don't need a balcony to start

Làròu doesn't require a trip to China to taste — just a Chinese grocery store, a bit of curiosity, and a rice cooker.

  • Chinese grocery stores Look in the refrigerated or vacuum-packed section, often labeled 臘肉 or by region, e.g. "Sichuan-style bacon."
  • Order it cooked 臘味煲仔飯 (claypot rice with cured meats) is the easiest, most forgiving way to meet the flavor for the first time.
  • Make it at home Salt-cured, air-dried versions are approachable for a first attempt — cold, dry weather and patience matter more than equipment.
